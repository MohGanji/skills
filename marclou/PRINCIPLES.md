# The 32 Principles -- Deep Treatment

Marc Lou's internal checklist for a product that goes viral and sells. Each is a hard-won rule from shipping profitable products solo, not a textbook chapter. Use the reasoning as an intuition pump for spotting what to fix. Ordered by lens, not by rank -- inside each lens the highest-leverage rule comes first.

---

## Pricing & money

### 1. No free plan
Free users are leeches. They inflate support load and server costs, and they push you to build features your *paying* customers never asked for. Less than 3% of free users ever convert. A free plan feels generous and quietly bleeds you. **Remove it.** If you need a taste, give a live demo or a sample on the landing page (see #25) -- not an account.

### 8. Hard paywall
Signups don't pay the bills. If nobody will pull out a credit card, you don't have validation -- you have a hobby with traffic. **Ask for payment before you ask for data.** A hard paywall tells you in week one whether the thing is real. Emails collected are not proof; charges are.

### 12. Popcorn pricing -- three choices
Your visitor came to buy, not to study a spreadsheet. Every extra tier is another decision and another reason to close the tab. Keep it to three: **Good. Better. Best.** Anchor with the Best, sell the middle, and let the cheap one make the middle look smart. More than three columns and you've turned a purchase into homework.

### 16. Pricing in the header
The pricing section is one of the *first* places visitors look -- they use it to understand what the product even is, not just what it costs. Hiding it reads as "if you have to ask." **Put "Pricing" in the nav.** Make it impossible to miss.

### 27. No subscription
People already pay for too many subscriptions and resent the next one. A monthly charge is a monthly chance to cancel and a monthly support of "how do I turn this off." **One-time payments are 10x easier to sell.** Only go recurring if you literally cannot ship the product without ongoing cost (real per-user infra, not "MRR looks nice").

### 32. Charge more than competitors
Nobody talks about the second-cheapest option. Being cheap is not a moat -- it's a race you lose to someone with more runway. A higher price signals confidence, filters out the leeches, and gives you margin to over-deliver. **Charge more.** The people who balk at the price were never going to be good customers.

## Hero & first screen

### 20. Sold from the hero alone
80% of visitors never scroll past the hero. If they don't understand the product and want it within a few seconds, you've already lost -- everything below the fold is for the 20%. **Fix the hero first, before anything else on the page.** The hero is the product to most people.

### 6. One idea per screen
Don't say everything at once. Each screen should land exactly one idea and nothing else -- like a single Instagram post, not a cluttered newspaper. Every extra element competing for attention means the main thing gets less. **One screen, one message.**

### 10. Show before you explain
A demo communicates more than three paragraphs ever will. A GIF, a 20-second screen recording, a live embed -- put the product *doing the thing* above the fold. **Show, don't tell.** People believe what they see and skim what they read.

### 25. Let them play before they pay
Don't lock your best feature behind the signup. Put a working slice right on the landing page -- a live input, a sample output, a playground. Letting people *feel* the value before paying is the most honest conversion tool there is. **Play before pay.**

## Headline & words

### 7. A fifth grader understands it
Complexity kills curiosity. The moment a reader has to re-read a sentence, they're gone. Use small words your mum would get. **If it needs a second read, it's too complex.** Simple isn't dumb -- it's respect for a distracted reader.

### 17. Remembered the next day
Write five headlines. Show them to friends. Wait 24 hours and ask which one they remember. Keep that one, kill the rest. A headline that doesn't survive a day won't survive a scroll. **Test for memory, not for clever.**

### 18. Emotional headline
People don't remember features -- they remember feelings. A great headline makes someone laugh, say "wow," or think "what the hell is this?" Flat and factual gets scrolled past. **Write for humans, aim for a reaction.**

### 3. Numbers, not adjectives
"Fast" is forgettable. "Save 4 hours every week" isn't. Numbers are concrete, believable, and picturable; adjectives are wallpaper. Everywhere you wrote "powerful," "simple," "affordable," find the number underneath it. **Make it countable.**

### 26. No weak words
"Most," "many," "rarely," "usually" -- nobody knows what they mean, so they mean nothing. Weak words hedge, and hedging reads as unsure. **Make statements, not estimates.** A clear claim can be pictured, remembered, and even argued with -- which is far better than ignored.

### 30. Under 10 words
If you can't explain the product in one short sentence, your users won't either -- and word of mouth dies. The one-liner is the unit of virality. **Get it under 10 words** before you worry about anything else on the page.

### 28. CTA that says what happens next
"Get Started" means nothing. "Analyze My Website," "Generate My Logo," "Find My Typos" tells people exactly what they're about to do and removes the flinch before a click. **Name the action, kill the uncertainty.**

### 22. One call to action
Every extra button creates hesitation. Given multiple paths, many people freeze and pick none. Pick the single next step and repeat it down the page. **One CTA.** Just one.

## Positioning & focus

### 11. Does one thing
The more your product does, the less anyone remembers it for. Nobody remembers a Swiss Army knife -- they remember the one tool that solved their problem. **Be known for one thing.** Every extra feature dilutes the sentence people use to recommend you.

### 24. Sells a desire, not a feature
People buy more money, more time, better health, more status, or less pain. Features are just the vehicle. "AI-powered dashboard" is a feature; "know exactly where your money goes" is a desire. **Sell the outcome, not the mechanism.**

### 21. Empathy before selling
Before anyone trusts your solution, they need to believe you understand their problem. Describe the pain better than they could describe it themselves, and they'll assume you have the fix. **Earn the sale by naming the problem first.**

### 13. Rides a wave
Build around a trend, technology, or problem people are already talking about. The wave does half your marketing -- the conversation already exists and you just join it. **Find the wave, then build the surfboard.** Fighting for attention with no wave is the hard way.

### 19. Never seen before
Nobody shares another clone. If it looks like ten things people have already seen, there's no reason to talk about it. Surprise people -- a mechanic, an angle, a visual, a claim they haven't encountered. **Do something new enough to be worth a screenshot.**

### 23. A name people remember
Use words people already know. Avoid wordplay, invented words, and anything that needs to be spelled out loud. A name people can't say, spell, or recall can't spread. **Pick a name that survives being told to a friend once.**

### 31. Comparison table
People don't care what your product does -- they care why they should switch. Show a simple table against the alternatives, using the features *your customers* actually weigh. Make the choice obvious so they don't have to do the comparison themselves. **Do the switching math for them.**

## Proof & author

### 9. Copy only you could write
If a competitor could copy-paste your landing page onto their site and it would still make sense, your copy is too generic. Write from real experience -- the specific frustration, the exact moment, the thing only someone who lived it would know. **Make your page impossible to steal.**

### 14. Steal copy from customers
Your customers already describe the product better than you do. Mine support tickets, reviews, DMs, and calls for the exact words they use, then put those words on the page. **Write like your customers talk** -- they wrote your best headline already.

### 15. Founder people can see and hear
People buy from people. A 60-second screen recording of the founder explaining the thing beats a polished corporate promo or a wall of features every time. **Show your face and your voice.** Trust transfers through a human, not a logo.

### 29. Testimonials before traffic
A landing page with no testimonials asks strangers to trust you blindly. Get a handful of real users, friends, or beta testers first and collect their words. **Collect proof before you send traffic** -- an empty wall of trust converts nobody.

## Design & shareability

### 2. Three colors
Every color fights for attention; the more you add, the less anyone notices what matters. Black text, white background, and one accent color for the Buy button. **Three colors, and the accent points at the money.** A rainbow page hides its own call to action.

### 4. A footer people want to share
97% of visitors won't buy -- but they might share, and people remember what they see last. Finish strong: a footer with personality, a punchline, a bold link, something screenshot-worthy. **Don't fade out; end on something worth passing along.**

### 5. OG image as a YouTube thumbnail
Your OG image is often seen more than your actual website -- it's the thumbnail in every share, tweet, and DM. If they don't click, they don't watch. So design it like a top YouTuber designs a thumbnail: big text, high contrast, one clear promise, a face if you can. **Treat the OG image as the real front door.**
